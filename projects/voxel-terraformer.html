VoxelTerraformer

A voxel engine written in C# on raylib — no game engine underneath — and four games built on it. It started as an experiment in streaming terrain and grew into a playground for one idea: one world, several ways of representing and editing it, from whole blocks to smooth, fully sculptable ground.

Four games, one engine

The engine takes care of the world, terrain generation, chunk streaming, meshing, rendering, input, UI and audio. The games are separate projects on top of it, switchable at any time from the pause menu.

The four games
GameWhat you do
Free WalkThe sandbox. Build, dig, switch between the three voxel modes. Mountains and caves reach 256 blocks in height; there is a jetpack.
Rocket StormSurvive waves of rockets — standard, cluster, buster and seeker — in smooth mode. Dig cover, shoot them down with the flak, climb the local leaderboard.
Solar SystemPlanets two to four kilometres across with atmospheres, rings, an asteroid belt and real gravity. Fly in six degrees of freedom and dive into an atmosphere. Two of the cores are molten.
Cave DiveAn underground world in the dark, smooth mode only. A lantern to see by, lamps to leave as markers, crystals to dig out of the walls.

Three modes, one world

The interesting part is that the mode is a rendering and editing decision, not a world format. The voxel data underneath never changes shape, so switching is free and reversible: build a house out of blocks, flip to smooth, and it looks like it was shaped out of clay. Flip back and every block is exactly where you left it.

The switch itself is visible: the new mode spreads across the terrain as a coloured ring, rebuilding the nearest chunks first, and the ring only advances as far as the terrain has actually been remeshed — so it doubles as a progress bar for the background workers.

Why raycasting is the hard part

Placing a block is easy once you know which block the cursor is pointing at, and that is the part that has to be exact. A ray stepped in fixed increments either misses thin geometry or costs far too many samples at distance. The engine instead walks the voxel grid cell by cell, so the block under the crosshair is identified exactly, at any range, and placement lands on the correct adjacent face rather than one voxel off.

How it scales

The world is divided into chunks 32 blocks wide and up to 256 blocks tall. Chunks that hold nothing but sky or solid rock are skipped outright. Around the player, chunks are generated on worker threads as they come into range, and a chunk is meshed once its neighbours exist, so the world grows at the edges without the frame noticing.

Near chunks are split into four sections and rendered in full detail; an edit rebuilds only the sections it touched, and those rebuilds jump the queue so digging never stalls. Chunks further out are merged into 2 m and 4 m sections instead, which keeps the amount of geometry in check as the view distance grows. Rebuilding a chunk mesh is expensive, and marching cubes makes it more so — doing that inline would turn every edit into a visible hitch, so all of it runs off the render frame and the finished mesh is handed back when it is ready.

The architecture is the point

World logic, rendering and utilities are kept strictly apart. The world does not know how it is drawn, and the renderer does not mutate world state. That separation is what makes both the chunking and the three modes tractable: a chunk can be regenerated, remeshed or skipped, and a second mesher can be bolted on beside the first, without any of that leaking into game logic.

The engine builds as one library; each game is its own project that references the engine and cannot see the other games. A game is a class with a [GameDefinition] attribute, and the launcher finds them by that attribute — adding a fifth means a new folder and one derived class. The engine's pure logic is covered by xunit tests that run without a window.

raylib was chosen deliberately over a full engine: it hands you a window, an input queue and a draw call, and nothing else. Everything above that — chunk meshing, the terrain generator, the raycast, the marching-cubes pass, the orbital gravity — had to be written, which was the entire point of the exercise.
