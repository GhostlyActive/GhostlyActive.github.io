VoxelTerraformer

An experimental voxel world in C#, focused on an endless streaming terrain, real-time terraforming and a clean, extensible architecture. The goal was never a finished game. It is a technical sandbox for working out how voxel engines hold together.

What it does

  • Endless world that streams in around you, with manual save and load
  • Three voxel modes over one terrain, switchable mid-play
  • Terrain meshed in the background, so building and digging never stall the frame
  • Ambient occlusion, a day/night cycle, distance fog, stars and drifting clouds
  • Sub-voxel collision, so you can walk into the holes you drilled
  • Live tuning menu for movement, gravity and brush settings

Three modes, one world

The interesting part is that the mode is a rendering and editing decision, not a world format. The voxel data underneath never changes shape, so switching is free and reversible: build a house out of blocks, flip to smooth, and it looks like it was shaped out of clay. Flip back and every block is exactly where you left it.

Why raycasting is the hard part

Placing a block is easy once you know which block the cursor is pointing at, and that is the part that has to be exact. A ray stepped in fixed increments either misses thin geometry or costs far too many samples at distance. The engine instead walks the voxel grid cell by cell, so the block under the crosshair is identified exactly, at any range, and placement lands on the correct adjacent face rather than one voxel off.

Meshing off the frame

Rebuilding a chunk mesh is expensive, and marching cubes makes it more so. Doing that work inline would turn every edit into a visible hitch, so meshing runs on background threads and the finished mesh is handed back to the renderer when it is ready. The same applies to the chunks streaming in ahead of the player: the world grows at the edges without the frame noticing.

The architecture is the point

World logic, rendering and utilities are kept strictly apart. The world does not know how it is drawn, and the renderer does not mutate world state. That separation is what makes both the chunking and the three modes tractable: a chunk can be regenerated, remeshed or skipped, and a second mesher can be bolted on beside the first, without any of that leaking into game logic.

raylib was chosen deliberately over a full engine: it hands you a window, an input queue and a draw call, and nothing else. Everything above that — chunk meshing, the terrain generator, the raycast, the marching-cubes pass — had to be written, which was the entire point of the exercise.
